Traceability Audits Reach Thermal Break Strip Marking — and Most Plants Cannot Prove What They Marked

📅 November 18, 2026 ⏱️ 5 min read

BEIJING, November 18, 2026 — Thermal break strip producers are being drawn into a documentation chain they did not build. As window-system manufacturers tighten supplier qualification for European and North American markets, they are passing the requirement back down the line: not only must the strip be marked with the correct grade, profile code and traceability number, the producer must be able to show how that mark was produced, with which parameters, on which batch, and at what point in the process the marking configuration was set.

KINGVAN TECHNOLOGY CO., LTD., a Beijing-based manufacturer of automatic laser marking systems for thermal insulation strips, reports that this requirement is now appearing in supplier questionnaires across Southeast Asia, Eastern Europe and the Middle East. The producers raising it are rarely failing audits for mark quality. They are failing them for evidence.

"An audit team will accept a mark that reads clearly," said a KINGVAN product engineer. "What they will not accept is an operator saying it always reads clearly. Once traceability is in scope, the marking station is no longer a printing device. It is a process record, and it needs to behave like one."

The Gap Between a Good Mark and a Controlled Process

In KINGVAN's reviews of existing installations, the weakness is almost never the laser or the mark itself. It is the connection between the mark and the batch record — a link that in many plants is made by hand, after the fact, by whoever is closing the shift.

The pattern is familiar. Mark content is entered or loaded at the controller at the start of a batch. Position is set by gauge or by a test mark on offcut. Parameters are adjusted until contrast looks right on the grade being run. When the batch closes, a line is written into a log sheet or a spreadsheet. Nothing in that sequence is wrong in itself, but four independent human actions sit between the process and the record, and any of them can be performed correctly while still producing a record that does not reflect what the machine actually did.

Where a customer's audit asks which parameters were used on a specific batch, or which job revision produced the mark on a sample strip returned months later, the plant has to reconstruct rather than retrieve. That reconstruction is where suppliers lose time — and sometimes lose qualification.

Recipes as the Unit of Record

KINGVAN's answer is structural rather than procedural. On the company's automatic systems, a batch is stored as a named job: mark content, position offset, marking parameters and the speed relationship for the profile all live together as one recallable entry, rather than being re-entered by an operator each time.

Because the job is the unit, the record follows it. A recall is a discrete event with a timestamp and an identity — not an inference from a log sheet. When a customer asks how a particular batch was marked, the answer is the job that was recalled and the machine settings it carried, not a best recollection of what a shift did.

"The difference matters most when things go wrong," the product engineer said. "If a customer returns strip with a weak or offset mark, a plant with recipes can pull the job and see whether the parameters were the ones intended for that grade. A plant without them has to guess whether the mark drifted, whether the wrong revision was loaded, or whether the strip itself changed. That argument takes weeks. The recipe takes minutes."

Parameters Held Per Job, Not Per Machine

Contrast is the second place where documentation and quality intersect. PA66 GF25 strip varies in glass-fibre content, surface finish and colour between grades and between suppliers, and a single set of marking parameters rarely produces identical contrast across the range. A plant that runs several grades on one line is, in effect, running several processes on one machine.

Holding parameters per job rather than per machine lets each grade carry the settings that were validated for it, and lets a changeover move between them without an operator re-tuning by eye. Practically, this converts contrast from a judgement into a specification — one that can be stated, repeated and, importantly, audited.

Position behaves the same way. Where the mark sits relative to the strip edge or a downstream cut reference is dictated by the customer's assembly process, and different customers specify different offsets. On a line handling several profiles, an offset stored against the job removes the step that most often produces a rejected first length, and it removes the operator-dependent variation that auditors look for.

What the Marking Station Has to Be Built to Do

KINGVAN's marking stations are built around a fiber laser source with no consumables — no ink, no solvent, no print head to clean — which removes a category of process drift that inkjet-based systems carry into audits: ink batch changes, print-quality verification after pauses, and head maintenance records. A mark produced by a fiber source does not change character because a cartridge was replaced.

The systems synchronise marking with the line's movement so that the mark is placed consistently at line speed, and they are supplied in four traction configurations — Single Side / Single Traction, Single Side / Double Traction, Double Side / Single Traction and Double Side / Double Traction — matched to how the strip is actually pulled on the line. For producers running double-sided identification, the configuration determines how both faces are handled within a single cycle rather than as two separate passes.

The company's systems carry CE certification and are designed for unattended operation across a shift, a requirement that matters as much to traceability as to throughput: a station that needs an operator to attend it between batches is a station whose settings are being touched, and every touch is a point where the record and the reality can diverge.

Preparing for the First Audit

KINGVAN recommends that producers asked to demonstrate traceability prepare four things before discussing equipment: the list of marks and content fields each customer requires, the position tolerance and reference feature each specifies, the grades and surface conditions the line actually runs, and the form in which the audit team wants the batch record delivered.

The last item is the one most often missing, and the one that determines the design. A customer who wants a batch record delivered as a job listing is satisfied by recipe recall. A customer who wants a per-length record of what was marked needs the marking station integrated with the line's own counting or the plant's production system. Those are different specifications, and the difference is cheap to resolve before the order and expensive afterwards.

"Traceability is usually described as a documentation problem," the product engineer concluded. "In practice it is a control problem that produces documentation as a by-product. Get the control right — stored jobs, validated parameters, a station that runs the same way every shift — and the file the auditor wants already exists. Everything else is printing it out."
