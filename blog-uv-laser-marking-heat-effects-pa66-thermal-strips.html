UV Laser Marking Heat Effects on PA66 GF25 Thermal Strips: Controlling Surface Damage

📅 November 2, 2026 ⏱️ 10 min read

Published: November 2, 2026

When a thermal break strip comes out of the marking station with a slightly glossy edge line, a faint brown tint, or a tolerance that has shifted more than it used to, the first reaction is usually to blame the laser source. In most cases the source is fine. The strip was heated — locally, briefly, and repeatedly — and the material responded the way polyamide 66 with 25% glass fiber always responds to heat.

UV laser marking is chosen for PA66 GF25 because it delivers high-contrast marks with far less thermal load than infrared sources. That does not make it a zero-heat process. This article explains where the residual heat goes, what it does to the polymer, and how to specify and run a UV marking system so that the mark stays permanent without the strip changing character underneath it.

Why PA66 GF25 Reacts to Localized Heat

Polyamide 66 is semi-crystalline. Its properties depend not only on the chemistry but on the crystal structure — how much of the polymer is crystalline, what form those crystals take, and how the glass fibers are wetted by the matrix. Heat changes all three:

  • Annealing and crystallinity shift. Holding PA66 near its glass transition region long enough allows the amorphous fraction to reorganize. Dimensionally, parts tend to shrink slightly and become more brittle. A strip is a long, thin, extruded profile — small local shrinkage shows up as bow or twist, not as a visible crack.
  • Moisture migration. Polyamides are hygroscopic. Local heating drives moisture out of the marked zone. The zone dries, contracts relative to the surrounding material, and can develop micro-stress at the boundary. In ambient service the moisture comes back; the stress history does not always disappear with it.
  • Glass fiber / matrix interface. The 25% glass fiber content is what makes PA66 GF25 stiff and dimensionally stable — and it is the weakest link thermally. Differential expansion between fiber and matrix under repeated local heating cycles can loosen the interface in the near-surface layer.
  • Surface oxidation. Excess heat at the surface produces yellowing or browning that is cosmetic on its own, but it is a reliable indicator that more energy was deposited than the process needed.

The good news: UV marking works on this material specifically because the mechanism is largely photochemical. Short-wavelength pulses break bonds at the surface and produce the contrast through carbonization-confined-to-the-surface and structural change in a very shallow layer, rather than by bulk ablation. The bad news: that only holds if the process parameters keep the thermal component small. Poorly tuned UV parameters behave much more like a heat process than like a photochemical one.

What "Excess Heat" Actually Looks Like on a Strip

Field feedback on thermal strip lines converges on a familiar set of symptoms. Each points at a different part of the process rather than at the laser itself:

Symptom Likely cause Where to look first
Brown or yellow tint around the mark Excess energy density per pulse Pulse energy and repetition rate
Glossy or melted edge on the character Spot too large / focal plane off / feed too slow Focal distance, spot size, marking speed
Mark fine at start of profile, weak at end Focal drift along the strip or focus instability Strip height variation, roller runout, lens focus setting
Slight bow or twist after marking Cumulative thermal input, usually repeated passes Pass count, overlap, and whether the process can run in one pass
Hairline cracking at the mark edge after ageing Localized moisture loss plus stress concentration Energy density; also check conditioning before marking
Mark contrast fades in a warm store Mark depth too shallow — thermal-driven change without surface conversion Verified against the retained reference sample

Controlling the Thermal Load: The Four Parameters That Matter

The UV marking process gives you four controls that govern heat input per unit area. Get them right and the thermal load is negligible; get any one badly wrong and you are effectively running a heat-treating process on the edge of your strip.

1. Pulse energy and repetition rate

Low pulse energy at high repetition rate is the correct regime for polymer marking. It deposits many small, low-fluence events at the surface instead of a few high-fluence shocks. If you find yourself raising pulse energy to recover contrast, stop and check the optics first — a hazed protective window or a dirty field lens will reduce contrast, and the instinctive fix of "more power" is precisely the change that introduces surface damage.

2. Spot size and focal distance

Spot size sets the area over which the energy is deposited. An oversized spot delivers more total energy per unit area than the process needs and starts to melt rather than convert the surface. Confirm the focal distance at which the system is specified, and then confirm that the strip path actually holds that distance. On a strip line, roller runout and traction belt tension change the strip height by small amounts that matter at focus.

3. Marking speed and overlap

Speed and repetition rate together determine pulse overlap. Too much overlap delivers repeated energy to the same spot — the classic path to gloss, brownish edges, and cumulative heating. Tune overlap down until contrast is marginal, then step back up slightly. That point is usually where the mark is permanent and the strip is untouched.

4. Pass count

A single pass is always preferable on PA66 GF25. Two passes double the thermal exposure and, because the second pass hits an already-modified surface, tend to widen the affected zone rather than deepen the mark. If your process needs two passes to achieve contrast, the parameter set is wrong somewhere upstream — usually optics cleanliness or focus.

Practical rule: if the mark looks better when you increase power and worse when you increase speed, you are in the wrong regime. On this material you should be able to increase speed without losing acceptance, and increasing power should not be necessary to reach it.

Verification That Catches Thermal Effects Before the Customer Does

A mark that passes visual inspection on the line can still fail after a season in a frame. Build verification into the process rather than into the complaint handling:

  1. Retained reference sample. Keep a marked strip of each production batch, stored with the batch record, and compare new output to it under the same lighting. This catches optical drift and contrast fade — the two slowest and most common failure modes.
  2. Dimensional check on the marked zone. Measure strip width and flatness across a marked section against an unmarked section from the same extrusion. A consistent offset means cumulative thermal input.
  3. Mark depth check. Depth should stay in a narrow, repeatable band. A drift toward deeper marks is a drift toward more heat.
  4. Accelerated ageing on a coupon. Where the end application involves heat or humidity exposure, run a sample through the relevant ageing condition and re-inspect for edge cracking, gloss change, and contrast retention.
  5. Chiller and optics log. Coolant temperature and optics condition correlate with process stability. When they drift, mark quality drifts with them — and operators compensate with power, which is the failure path described above.

Specifying a UV System for Thermal Strip Production

For buyers specifying a line, the thermal question should appear in the procurement conversation explicitly. Ask:

  • What is the recommended parameter window for PA66 GF25 on this configuration, and what is the evidence for it — reference installations, sample marks, or process trials?
  • What marking speed is achievable in a single pass at the required mark depth? Any answer that quietly assumes two passes is a different thermal process.
  • How stable is the focus across the full strip length, including strip height tolerance and roller runout?
  • What maintenance protects contrast so that operators do not compensate with power — and how is that maintenance scheduled?
  • For multi-head configurations, can each head be tuned independently? Two heads marking opposing faces on different strip lots will not share the same optimum.

The configuration choice itself matters. A Single Side, Single Traction line has one optical path and one heat source per strip; a Double Side, Double Traction line has two heads marking both faces, and the strip experiences heat from each side. The thermal budget per face does not change, but the net heat in the strip does, and on thin profiles that is worth checking against the flatness and bow requirement rather than assuming it is negligible.

The Bottom Line

UV laser marking on PA66 GF25 produces permanent, high-contrast marks because it avoids bulk heating — not because heat is absent. The process is forgiving of a well-tuned set of parameters and unforgiving of a badly tuned one, and the difference shows up months later in the field rather than in the first inspection.

Treat the thermal load as a process variable you control, verify it with retained samples and simple dimensional checks, and keep optics maintenance ahead of the contrast curve. Then you will not be the line that discovers its UV process was a heat process the whole time, one warranty claim at a time.
